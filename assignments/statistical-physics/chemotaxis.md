A swimming bacterium alternates between straight *runs* and abrupt changes of direction called *tumbles*.
By tumbling less often when things are getting better, it can move towards food without ever measuring where the food is.
In this assignment you start from the simplest random walk, connect it to diffusion, add persistent swimming, and finally give your swimmers a short memory of the nutrient concentration.

*How can random microscopic motion produce diffusion, persistence, and directed chemotactic motion?*

This is a minimal statistical model, not a detailed description of a bacterium: the particles move on a line, do not interact, and do not consume the nutrients.

## Part 1: A random walk

A walker starts at $q_0 = 0$ and at every step moves a distance $a$ left or right with equal probability:

$$
q_{n+1} = q_n + a\,\xi_n, \qquad \Pr(\xi_n = +1) = \Pr(\xi_n = -1) = \tfrac12 .
$$

For an ensemble of independent walkers, with $\langle \cdot \rangle$ the average over walkers, theory predicts

$$
\langle q_n \rangle = 0, \qquad \langle q_n^2 \rangle = n a^2 .
$$

- Generate a long sequence of random directions $\xi_n$ and check that the fractions of $+1$ and $-1$ are close to one half.
- With $a = 1$, plot one trajectory against step number, then a few more generated by the same rule.
- Simulate $N = 10\,000$ independent walkers for 200 steps, storing the positions as one NumPy array.
  Plot $\langle q_n \rangle$ and the mean-squared displacement (MSD) $\langle q_n^2 \rangle$ against $n$, together with the predictions, and plot histograms of the positions after a few selected numbers of steps.
- Repeat the final-step estimates of $\langle q \rangle$ and $\langle q^2 \rangle$ with $N = 1000$ and with a different random seed, and compare how much they change.

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-walk

Why can a single trajectory seem to drift strongly even though the walk is unbiased?
Why does the mean position stay near zero while the MSD grows?
Which of your estimates became more reliable with more walkers, and by roughly how much?
:::

## Part 2: From random walks to diffusion

Give each step a duration $\Delta\tau$, so that $\tau = n\,\Delta\tau$.
Then $\langle q^2 \rangle = 2 D \tau$ with diffusion coefficient $D = a^2 / (2\Delta\tau)$, and at long times the position distribution approaches the Gaussian

$$
\rho(q, \tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D\tau}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{q^2}{4 D \tau}\right),
$$

which solves the diffusion equation $\partial \rho / \partial \tau = D\,\partial^2 \rho / \partial q^2$.

- Estimate $D$ from the slope of your MSD and compare it with $a^2/(2\Delta\tau)$.
- Simulate a second walk with $a = 0.5$ and $\Delta\tau = 0.25$, which has the same $D$ as $a = \Delta\tau = 1$.
  At the same *physical* times, an early one ($\tau = 2$) and a late one ($\tau = 100$), compare normalised position histograms of both walks with $\rho(q, \tau)$.
- Solve the diffusion equation with the FTCS method from [](#partial-differential-equations-2_page), starting from all the probability in the central grid cell, i.e. a density $1/h$ there and zero elsewhere.
  Use grid spacing $h = 0.25$, a domain $-70 \leq q \leq 70$, and a timestep giving $r = D\,\Delta\tau_\mathrm{PDE}/h^2 = 0.4$.
  At $\tau = 100$, compare the FTCS density with the Gaussian and with the walker histogram, and check that the FTCS density stays non-negative and normalised to one.

:::{dropdown} Hint: histograms of a lattice walk
After $n$ steps a walker can only be at sites separated by $2a$: at even $n$ the positions are even multiples of $a$.
Choose histogram bins that are $2a$ wide and centred on those sites; with `density=True`, the histogram is then a probability per unit length that can be compared with $\rho$.
:::

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-diffusion

Which microscopic details of the walk disappear in the diffusion description, and why can different step sizes and step times give the same large-scale motion?
Why does the Gaussian describe your histograms much better at $\tau = 100$ than at $\tau = 2$?
:::

## Part 3: Persistent swimming

Real bacteria do not take independent steps: they swim at a constant speed $v$ and only change direction now and then.
In our one-dimensional run-and-tumble model, a particle moves at speed $v$ and *reverses* its direction at random times, with rate $\lambda_0$.
Measuring time in units of $1/\lambda_0$ and length in units of $v/\lambda_0$, the dimensionless position obeys $dq/d\tau = s$ with $s = \pm 1$, and reversals happen at rate $\Lambda = 1$.

The time between reversals is then exponentially distributed, $p(u) = e^{-u}$, and in a timestep $\Delta\tau$ the probability of at least one reversal is $1 - e^{-\Lambda\Delta\tau}$.
A simple simulation moves every particle by $s\,\Delta\tau$ and then reverses it with that probability; this is accurate when $\Lambda\Delta\tau \ll 1$.

- Start $N = 10\,000$ particles at $q = 0$ with random initial directions, use $\Delta\tau = 0.02$, and run to $\tau = 30$.
  Plot a few trajectories and compare them with Part 1.
- Measure the durations of completed runs and compare their histogram with $e^{-u}$.
- Plot the MSD on log-log axes against the continuous-time prediction
  $$
  \left\langle [q(\tau) - q(0)]^2 \right\rangle = \tau - \frac{1 - e^{-2\tau}}{2},
  $$
  which grows as $\tau^2$ at short times and approaches $\tau$ (that is, $D_\mathrm{eff} = 1/2$) at long times.
- Repeat with $\Delta\tau = 0.01$ and compare the MSD at a few times, taking the sampling uncertainty into account.

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-persistence

Why is the short-time MSD proportional to $\tau^2$?
What makes the particles forget their initial direction, and how does the time at which the MSD changes behaviour relate to the typical run duration?
What information about the trajectories is lost in the long-time diffusion description?
:::

## Part 4: Swimming towards food

Now confine the swimmers to a channel $0 \leq q \leq Q$ with reflecting ends: a particle that reaches a wall reverses its direction (this is not a tumble) and any overshoot is reflected back into the channel.
The channel holds a fixed nutrient profile, highest at the right end:

$$
c(q) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{(Q - q)^2}{2 w^2}\right].
$$

Each particle compares the concentration it feels now with the one it felt a time $\tau_m$ ago, and tumbles less often if things have improved:

$$
\Delta c(\tau) = c\big(q(\tau)\big) - c\big(q(\tau - \tau_m)\big), \qquad \Lambda(\tau) = e^{-\chi\,\Delta c(\tau)} .
$$

The sensing strength $\chi \geq 0$ sets how strongly it responds; for $\tau < \tau_m$ take $\Delta c = 0$.

The sensing rule changes how long runs last, not how fast the particle swims or which way it turns: every tumble is still a reversal.
:::

- Use $Q = 10$, $w = 4$, $\tau_m = 1$, $\Delta\tau = 0.02$ and $N = 5000$ particles, all starting uniformly in $4.5 \leq q \leq 5.5$ with random directions, and run to $\tau = 80$.
  Store each particle's concentration history so that you can look up the value one memory time ago; choosing $\tau_m$ as a whole number of steps makes this easy.
- For $\chi = 0$, $2$ and $4$, plot a few trajectories, the mean position $\langle q(\tau) \rangle$, the mean concentration $\langle c(\tau) \rangle$, and histograms of the positions at a few late times.
- Check your result for $\chi = 4$: repeat it once with a different seed and once with $\Delta\tau = 0.01$ (keeping $\tau_m = 1$), and compare the late-time mean position with the difference between $\chi = 4$ and $\chi = 0$.

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-chemotaxis

How can the ensemble move towards the food when no particle ever chooses the direction of the food?
Does the sensing rule change the swimming speed, the direction after a tumble, or the run durations?
Why does the mean position level off instead of growing forever?
How do your checks show that the effect is not a lucky random outcome or a timestep artefact?
:::

## Part 5: Open question

Use your chemotaxis simulation to investigate one question of your own.
State the question and a prediction before you calculate, compare three to five values of one parameter with everything else fixed, include the $\chi = 0$ reference, and add one check that your conclusion is robust.
Some possible directions:

- **Memory.**
  How does the response depend on the memory time $\tau_m$?
  What should happen for $\tau_m = 0$, and is a longer memory always better?
- **The shape of the food source.**
  How does the response depend on the width $w$ of the nutrient profile?
  Note that changing $w$ changes both the gradient and how much of the channel is rich in nutrients, so think carefully about what you measure.
- **Sensing the concentration itself.**
  Suppose a particle responded to the concentration it feels now, $\Lambda = e^{-\chi c(q)}$, instead of to its change.
  Would it accumulate near the food?

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-open

What did you investigate, and what did you predict?
Which result supports or contradicts your prediction, and what shows that the effect is larger than the statistical and numerical uncertainty?
:::

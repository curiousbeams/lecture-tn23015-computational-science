Many bacteria, such as *E. coli*, swim by rotating their flagella.
Their motion alternates between straight *runs*, lasting about a second, and short *tumbles*, in which the bacterium stops and sets off in a new direction.
A bacterium is far too small to compare the concentration of food at its front and its back, but it can compare the concentration *now* with what it was a moment ago.
By tumbling less often when things are getting better, it drifts towards food without ever measuring where the food is.
This is *chemotaxis*.

In this assignment you build up to that behaviour in four steps: the simplest random walk, its connection to diffusion, persistent swimming with random reversals, and finally swimmers with a short memory of the nutrient concentration.

*How can random microscopic motion produce diffusion, persistence, and directed chemotactic motion?*

This is a minimal statistical model, not a detailed description of a bacterium: the particles move along a line, with position $q$, they do not interact, and they do not consume the nutrients.

## Part 1: A random walk

A walker starts at position $q_0 = 0$ and at every step moves a distance $a$ left or right with equal probability:

$$
q_{n+1} = q_n + a\,\xi_n, \qquad \Pr(\xi_n = +1) = \Pr(\xi_n = -1) = \tfrac12 .
$$

For an ensemble of independent walkers, with $\langle \cdot \rangle$ the average over walkers, theory predicts

$$
\langle q_n \rangle = 0, \qquad \langle q_n^2 \rangle = n a^2 .
$$

$\langle q_n^2 \rangle$ is the *mean-squared displacement* (MSD), since every walker starts at $q = 0$.

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-walk

Use $a = 1$.

1. Generate a long sequence of random directions $\xi_n$, and verify that the fractions of $+1$ and $-1$ are close to one half.
2. Plot one trajectory $q_n$ against step number $n$, and then a few more generated by the same rule.
3. Simulate $N = 10\,000$ independent walkers for 200 steps, storing their positions as one NumPy array.
4. Plot $\langle q_n \rangle$ and the MSD against $n$, together with the predictions, and plot histograms of the positions after a few selected numbers of steps.
5. Compute $\langle q \rangle$ and $\langle q^2 \rangle$ after the last step for $N = 1000$ and for $N = 10\,000$, each with two different random seeds, and compare how much they change.
6. Explain why a single trajectory can seem to drift strongly even though the walk is unbiased, and why the mean position stays near zero while the MSD grows.
   Identify which of your estimates became more reliable with more walkers, and by roughly how much.
:::

## Part 2: From random walks to diffusion

Give each step a duration $\Delta\tau$, so that after $n$ steps the time is $\tau = n\,\Delta\tau$.
Then $\langle q^2 \rangle = 2 D \tau$ with diffusion coefficient $D = a^2 / (2\Delta\tau)$, and at long times the position distribution approaches the Gaussian

$$
\rho(q, \tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D\tau}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{q^2}{4 D \tau}\right),
$$

which solves the diffusion equation $\partial \rho / \partial \tau = D\,\partial^2 \rho / \partial q^2$.

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-diffusion

1. Find $D$ from the slope of your MSD from Part 1, taking $\Delta\tau = 1$, and compare it with $a^2/(2\Delta\tau)$.
2. Simulate a second walk with $a = 0.5$ and $\Delta\tau = 0.25$, which has the same $D$.
3. Plot normalised position histograms of both walks at the same *physical* times, an early one ($\tau = 2$) and a late one ($\tau = 100$), together with $\rho(q, \tau)$.
4. Solve the diffusion equation with the FTCS method from [](#partial-differential-equations-2_page), starting from all the probability in the central grid cell, i.e. a density $1/h$ there and zero elsewhere.
   Use grid spacing $h = 0.25$, a domain $-70 \leq q \leq 70$, and a timestep giving $r = D\,\Delta\tau_\mathrm{PDE}/h^2 = 0.4$.
5. Plot the FTCS density at $\tau = 100$ together with the Gaussian and the walker histogram, and verify that it stays non-negative and normalised to one.
6. Explain which microscopic details of the walk disappear in the diffusion description, and why different step sizes and step times can give the same large-scale motion.
   Explain why the Gaussian describes your histograms much better at $\tau = 100$ than at $\tau = 2$.
:::

After $n$ steps a walker can only be at sites separated by $2a$: at even $n$ the positions are even multiples of $a$.
Choose histogram bins that are $2a$ wide and centred on those sites; with `density=True`, the histogram is then a probability per unit length that can be compared with $\rho$.
:::

## Part 3: Persistent swimming

Real bacteria do not take independent steps: they swim at a constant speed $v$ and only change direction now and then.
In our one-dimensional *run-and-tumble* model, a particle swims at speed $v$ and, at random moments, *tumbles*: it reverses its direction.
A *run* is the straight stretch between two tumbles, and its *duration* is the time between them.

Tumbles happen at a *rate* $\lambda_0$: in a short time $dt$, a particle tumbles with probability $\lambda_0\,dt$, independently of what happened before.
Measuring time in units of $1/\lambda_0$ and length in units of $v/\lambda_0$, the dimensionless position obeys $dq/d\tau = s$, with direction $s = \pm 1$, and the dimensionless tumble rate is $\Lambda = 1$.
A run then lasts a random time $u$ with the exponential distribution $p(u) = e^{-u}$, so the mean run duration is 1.

You do not need to draw exponential random numbers to simulate this.
In a timestep $\Delta\tau$, the probability of at least one tumble is $1 - e^{-\Lambda\Delta\tau}$, so a simple simulation moves every particle by $s\,\Delta\tau$ and then reverses it with that probability.
The exponential run durations then come out by themselves.
This is accurate when $\Lambda\Delta\tau \ll 1$.

For particles that start at $q = 0$ with random directions, the continuous-time model predicts

$$
\left\langle [q(\tau) - q(0)]^2 \right\rangle = \tau - \frac{1 - e^{-2\tau}}{2} ,
$$

which grows as $\tau^2$ at short times and approaches $\tau$, that is an effective diffusion coefficient $D_\mathrm{eff} = 1/2$, at long times.

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-persistence

1. Simulate $N = 10\,000$ particles, starting at $q = 0$ with random directions, with $\Delta\tau = 0.02$ up to $\tau = 30$, and plot a few trajectories.
2. Measure the durations of completed runs, from one tumble to the next, and plot their histogram together with $e^{-u}$.
3. Plot the MSD on log-log axes together with the prediction.
4. Repeat the run with $\Delta\tau = 0.01$, and tabulate the MSD of both runs at a few times next to the prediction, with the standard errors over the particles.
5. Explain why the short-time MSD is proportional to $\tau^2$, what makes the particles forget their initial direction, and how the time at which the MSD changes behaviour relates to the mean run duration.
   Identify which information about the trajectories is lost in the long-time diffusion description.
:::

## Part 4: Swimming towards food

Now confine the swimmers to a channel $0 \leq q \leq Q$ with reflecting ends: a particle that reaches a wall reverses its direction (this is not a tumble), and any overshoot is reflected back into the channel.
The channel holds a fixed nutrient profile, highest at the right end:

$$
c(q) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{(Q - q)^2}{2 w^2}\right].
$$

Each particle compares the concentration it feels now with the one it felt a *memory time* $\tau_m$ ago, and tumbles less often if things have improved.
Its tumble rate is no longer the constant $\Lambda = 1$, but changes in time:

$$
\Delta c(\tau) = c\big(q(\tau)\big) - c\big(q(\tau - \tau_m)\big), \qquad \Lambda(\tau) = e^{-\chi\,\Delta c(\tau)} .
$$

$\Lambda(\tau)$ is the tumble rate at time $\tau$, in the units of Part 3: in a short timestep $\Delta\tau$, the particle tumbles with probability $1 - e^{-\Lambda(\tau)\Delta\tau}$.
The *sensing strength* $\chi \geq 0$ sets how strongly it responds; for $\tau < \tau_m$ take $\Delta c = 0$.
Particles with $\chi > 0$ are *chemotactic*: their tumbling responds to the nutrient.
With $\chi = 0$ they are *non-chemotactic*, tumble at the constant rate $\Lambda = 1$, and serve as the reference.
As [](#fig-sp2-channel) shows, sensing only changes how long runs last.

1. Run the simulation for $\chi = 0$, $2$ and $4$.
2. Plot a few trajectories, the mean position $\langle q(\tau) \rangle$, and the mean concentration $\langle c(\tau) \rangle$, for each $\chi$.
3. Plot histograms of the positions at a few late times.

5. Repeat the $\chi = 4$ run once with a different seed and once with $\Delta\tau = 0.01$ (keeping $\tau_m = 1$), and compare the change in its late-time mean position with the difference between $\chi = 4$ and $\chi = 0$.
6. Explain how the ensemble can move towards the food when no particle ever chooses the direction of the food, and which of swimming speed, direction after a tumble and run duration the sensing rule changes.
   Explain why the mean position levels off instead of growing forever, and how your checks show that the effect is not a lucky random outcome or a timestep artefact.

## Part 5: Open question

Use your chemotaxis simulation to investigate one question of your own, with everything except one parameter fixed, and always with the non-chemotactic $\chi = 0$ reference at the same settings.
Some possible directions:

- **Memory.**
  How does the response depend on the memory time, for example $\tau_m = 0$, $0.5$, $1$ and $2$ at $\chi = 4$?
  What should happen for $\tau_m = 0$, and is a longer memory always better?
- **The shape of the food source.**
  How does the response depend on the width of the nutrient profile, for example $w = 2$, $4$ and $8$?
  Changing $w$ changes both the gradient and how much of the channel is rich in nutrients, so think carefully about what you measure.
- **Sensing the concentration itself.**
  Suppose a particle responded to the concentration it feels now, $\Lambda = e^{-\chi c(q)}$, instead of to its change.
  Would it accumulate near the food?

:::{exercise}
:label: ex-sp2-open

1. Write down your question and your prediction before you calculate.
2. Compute the late-time mean position, or another measure you define, for three to five values of your parameter and for the $\chi = 0$ reference.
3. Plot your measure against the parameter, and position histograms for representative values.
4. Include one check that your conclusion is robust, for example a second seed or a smaller $\Delta\tau$.
5. Explain whether your results support your prediction, and what shows that the effect is larger than the statistical and numerical uncertainty.
:::
